                                               Abstract. Data sequences, such as time series, traces, and sensor
                                               streams, are words over large or infinite data domains. Their properties
                                               often depend on comparisons between values at different positions, which
                                               finite automata cannot express directly. We study synthesis and verifi-
                                               cation for Deterministic Register Automata (DRAs) over ordered data
                                               sequences. DRAs combine finite control with registers for remembered
                                               data values, providing compact symbolic descriptions of quantitative
                                               sequence languages. Our technical core is a local-search synthesis pro-
                                               cedure and an exact robustness checker for DRAs. We fix the alphabet
                                               theory to ordered rationals (Q; <, =) with rational guard constants
                                               and use non-negative affine accumulator costs. For a fixed number of
                                               DRA registers, the checker runs in polynomial time; its dependence on
                                               that number is exponential. It reduces robustness to coverability and
                                               then to a shortest-path problem. Since the search ranges over Q+ , it
                                               produces variable-length witnesses and, for RAA-definable alignment
                                               costs such as DTW, explores a counterexample space with no a priori
                                               length bound. As an application, we use learned DRAs as interpretable
                                               surrogates for queried neural sequence classifiers. A probably approxi-
                                               mately correct (PAC) validation loop refines rejected candidates, returns
                                               genuine non-robustness witnesses for the classifier when found, and ac-
                                               cepts a surrogate only with statistical guarantees for fidelity and local
                                               stability. We implement the framework in RAExc, enabling the synthe-
                                               sis of interpretable automata and the verification of their robustness.
                                               We evaluate the approach on benchmark languages and sequentialized
                                               MNIST, demonstrating high accuracy while verifying the robustness of
                                               the learned automata.


                                         1   Introduction
                                         Data sequences are words whose positions carry values from an infinite, or very
                                         large, data domain such as integers, rationals, or reals. They arise naturally in
                                         execution traces, sensor streams, event logs, financial indicators, and other time
                                         series data. Classical finite automata provide a canonical model of regular lan-
                                         guages over finite alphabets, but they cannot directly express properties whose
2       Hong et al.

truth depends on comparing data values across positions, such as whether suc-
cessive peaks increase, whether future values exceed remembered thresholds, or
whether a signal preserves a quantitative trend. This limitation motivates au-
tomata models over data words and data sequences, including data automata,
nominal automata, and register automata [11,9,42,10,8]. Register automata ex-
tend finite control with finitely many registers that store selected input values
and compare later inputs with those stored values or with constants. Over or-
dered domains such as (Q; <, =), they give a compact symbolic language for
global quantitative patterns while retaining useful algorithmic properties, with
applications in verification and synthesis [1,34,18].
    This paper studies the synthesis and verification of deterministic register
automata (DRAs) over numeric data sequences. On the synthesis side, we ask
how to construct a small DRA from labelled sequence data or from a classifier
that can be queried on examples. On the verification side, we ask whether the
language recognised by a DRA is locally stable around a given data sequence
under a quantitative perturbation cost. These two questions support one another:
synthesis produces an interpretable symbolic model, and verification makes that
model an object on which robustness questions can be answered exactly.
    A timely source of such queried classifiers is modern sequence modelling with
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), LSTMs, and transformers [25,52,55]. These
models are widely used on sequence data, but their internal decision procedures
are difficult to inspect. A popular way to explain them is therefore to extract
an automaton that approximates the trained network and then analyse that au-
tomaton instead of the original model. This idea has been studied for RNNs,
weighted automata, and transformers [54,47,41,56,2]. Existing extraction meth-
ods, however, predominantly target automata over finite alphabets. This is a
serious mismatch for numeric time series: discretising the values can obscure
comparisons between positions, while a finite automaton cannot remember and
compare data values in the way required by many natural quantitative rules.
DRAs are a natural replacement because their registers provide precisely the
finite memory needed for order and equality comparisons over data values.
    For an automaton to be a useful explanation, agreement with the neural
model is not enough. The explanation should also be stable under perturbations
that are small according to the intended semantics of the data. If a surrogate
changes its classification after an imperceptible or semantically insignificant edit,
then it may describe a brittle boundary artefact rather than a robust pattern. We
therefore seek robust automata explanations: compact DRAs that agree with the
queried model on the inputs of interest and whose classifications persist through
a neighbourhood of those inputs. The following example shows why numeric
comparisons call for DRAs and why robustness matters.

    The robustness question raised by Example 1 exposes two limitations of exist-
ing robustness checkers for neural sequence models [36,17,31,43,57,39,50,38,27].
First, most RNN checkers propagate bounds or abstractions through specific
recurrent cells, and transformer checkers reason about self-attention layers us-
ing convex relaxations, linear bounds, or path constraints. These methods can
provide formal guarantees for the analysed neural architecture, but they require
access to the model structure, parameters, and internal operations. Second, these
checkers typically fix the shape of the input tensor before verification. For se-
quence models, this means that the input length is bounded in advance: the
checker only perturbs the values of the original sequence.
    This fixed-length view is appropriate for length-preserving Lp perturbations
or for analyses with an explicit window bound, but it does not directly answer the
language-theoretic question whether there exists some data sequence w ∈ Q+ in
the neighbourhood of the original sequence that flips the label. The distinction
matters for edit and alignment distances. Under edit costs, counterexamples
may have lengths different from the original input. Under dynamic time warping
(DTW) and related alignment costs, the length of a potential counterexample is
not bounded by the original input length [46]. Thus, fixed-length neural checkers
can miss the very counterexamples that sequence level robustness is intended to
rule out.
    Our approach is to verify robustness on the extracted DRA itself. Perturba-
tion costs are specified by Register Automata with Accumulator (RAA), which
scan a reference sequence and a candidate perturbed sequence while accumulat-
ing their distance. Given a DRA A, a sequence v, and a cost function D rep-
resented by an RAA, local robustness asks whether Bδ (v) ∩ L(Ac ) = ∅, where
Bδ (v) = {w ∈ Q+ : D(v, w) < δ}. The search ranges over all data sequences
w ∈ Q+ rather than over a fixed-dimensional perturbation space. Consequently,
4      Hong et al.

the checker can search for witnesses of variable length, and for costs definable by
RAAs such as DTW and edit distance, it is not tied to any a priori bound on the
counterexample length. For a fixed number of registers, we reduce this robustness
problem to coverability for bounded projected RAAs and then to a single-source
shortest path computation. When robustness fails, the same construction yields
a concrete witness that flips the DRA label.
    It remains to justify why the verified DRA explains the neural model. Our
method uses the neural model only as a labelling oracle. It is therefore indepen-
dent of the model architecture: the same extraction and validation loop can be
applied to LSTMs, transformers, or any other classifier over numeric data se-
quences. The cost is that the guarantee transferred from the DRA to the neural
model is statistical rather than absolute. This is the standard model-agnostic
setting, where explanations are validated relative to a reference input distribu-
tion. Popular post-hoc explainers such as LIME, Anchors, and SHAP likewise
characterise model behaviour through background samples or perturbation dis-
tributions [48,49,40]. Our loop learns a compact DRA from labelled samples,
tests agreement with the neural model on fresh samples, and invokes the ex-
act DRA robustness checker on samples where the two models agree. A failed
robustness check either refines the learner or, if the neural model also flips on
the returned witness, produces a genuine witness that the neural model is not
robust. If the loop accepts, the DRA has PAC statistical guarantees for both
fidelity and local stability under the reference distribution [33,5].

– Robustness verification for DRAs over data sequences. We give a
  polynomial-time robustness checker for DRAs with a fixed number of registers
  over (Q; <, =), with perturbation costs specified by RAAs. Unlike fixed-length

  robustness checking in a validation loop that refines rejected candidates, re-

and is therefore accepted by A.

Learning. Suppose we have an accurate trading model N that classifies the two
sequences in Fig. 1 as uptrends. Recall that the right-hand sequence is unstable
under the last-letter distance: even an arbitrarily small perturbation (ε > 0) of
the final price violates the relevant running constraint and flips the signal. For
this sequence, our pipeline identifies a nearby sample as a witness of instability
and refrains from certifying a robust DRA for N . In contrast, the left-hand se-
quence is stable under the same metric—its signal remains unchanged unless the
last price falls below the previous low. Consequently, we can learn a robust DRA
for N over a neighbourhood of the left-hand sequence. By explicitly targeting
robustness, the learned automaton captures the intended pattern with a mar-
gin: agreed sequences are locally stable, spurious counterexamples are filtered,
and genuine non-robust witnesses are reported. This design turns the extracted
DRA into an interpretable surrogate that provides both agreement and stability
guarantees.


3    Preliminaries

We fix the ordered-rational alphabet theory (Q; <, =), with rational constants
in guards. Q+ denotes the non-empty rational sequences; registers initially hold
0.
Register automata. A k-register automaton (k-RA) is A = (Q, ∆, q0 , F ),
where Q is finite, q0 ∈ Q, F ⊆ Q, and ∆ is a finite set of transitions (p, φ, ψ, q).
It has registers r̄ = (r1 , . . . , rk ). A guard φ(r̄, curr) is a conjunction of atoms
x ∼ y, where ∼∈ {<, ≤, =, ̸=, ≥, >} and x, y ∈ r̄ ∪ {curr} ∪ Q. An assignment
ψ is a parallel update in which every ri receives a constant, a register value, or
the current symbol.
    A configuration is (p, ν) with p ∈ Q and ν : r̄ → Q. On reading a, the
transition (p, φ, ψ, q) is enabled exactly when φ(ν, a) holds; it changes (p, ν) to
(q, ψ(ν, a)). A word is accepted if its run from (q0 , 0) ends in F ; its language is
L(A). The RA is deterministic (a DRA) if at most one outgoing transition is en-
abled in every configuration. Missing transitions reject. We totalise a DRA, when
needed, by adding a non-accepting sink, and then complement it by swapping fi-
nal and non-final states. DRA languages are closed under Boolean operations [8];
write Ac for a DRA recognising Q+ \ L(A).
                               Synthesis and Verification of Register Automata          7

Register automata with accumulators. A k-register automaton with accu-
mulator (k-RAA) AD = (Q, ∆, q0 , F ) reads a pair (v, w) ∈ Q+ × Q+ with one
head per sequence and computes a cost. Its transitions (p, φ, ψ, χ, q, mov) ex-
tend those of RAs: guards and assignments may refer to the letters curr1 , curr2
under the two heads, mov ∈ {1, 2, both} names the head(s) that advance, and
χ : acc += a1 · curr1 + a2 · curr2 + b (a1 , a2 , b ∈ Q) is an affine accumulator incre-
ment, required to be non-negative whenever φ holds (so that the shortest-path
reduction of Sec. 4 has non-negative edges).
    A configuration (p, i, j, ν) records the state, both head positions and the
register valuation; the run starts in (q0 , 1, 1, 0). A transition is enabled if its guard
holds and every head it reads or advances is not yet exhausted; a transition that
ignores an exhausted head may still fire, which allows insertions and deletions at
the end of a sequence. It updates the registers, advances the heads in mov (the
others re-read their letter), and adds χ(vi , wj ) to the cost. A run is accepting if it
ends in a final state with both heads exhausted. The accumulator is write-only:
guards never test it. RAAs are non-deterministic, which lets them choose, e.g.,
an alignment of v and w; AD (v, w) is the minimum cost over all accepting runs
(∞ if there is none), and AD computes D if AD (v, w) = D(v, w) for all (v, w).
Registers store letters for later comparison, as in RAs; in Sec. 4, the registers of
the DRA under analysis become registers of a product RAA. Define

Robustness. For δ > 0, let Bδ (v) = {w ∈ Q+ : D(v, w) < δ}. A DRA language
L(A) is δ-robust at v ∈ L(A) iff Bδ (v) ∩ L(Ac ) = ∅. Hence robustness asks
whether a word of cost below δ can reach the complement language. The RAAs

4    Verifying Robustness of DRAs

In this section, we develop the decision procedure behind the following theorem.

Theorem 1. For any fixed number of registers k and any fixed perturbation-cost
RAA with non-negative updates, checking whether a k-DRA is robust at a given
sequence is decidable in polynomial time. Moreover, whenever robustness fails, a
concrete witness to non-robustness can also be constructed in polynomial time.

The polynomial is in the automaton, the sequence, and the finite constant set in-
duced by the instance. The graph constructed below has O(|v||Q||C|k+1 ) vertices
and O(|v||∆||C|k+2 ) edges, so the running time of our algorithm is exponential
in k. Some super-polynomial dependence on k is expected: robustness subsumes
DRA emptiness, which is PSPACE-complete when k is part of the input [15],
so no algorithm can be polynomial jointly in the automaton size and k unless
P = PSPACE.
8      Hong et al.

    Our proof proceeds in two reductions: robustness of a k-DRA to coverabil-
ity for a restricted class of RAA, bounded projected RAA, then coverability to
single-source shortest paths. Both reductions run in polynomial time, and since
all accumulator updates (hence all graph edges) are non-negative, Dijkstra’s al-
gorithm solves the shortest-path problem in polynomial time, giving Theorem 1.
    For the first reduction, we use coverability, a thresholded min-cost question
in the sense of cost-register automata over finite alphabets [3]; when accumulator
updates are identically zero, it collapses to the usual reachability problem for
register automata with linear arithmetic [13]. For an RAA AD , a sequence v,
and a constant δ ∈ Q, we say that AD is covered at v by δ if wt(AD , v) < δ.
The coverability problem asks, given AD , v, and δ, whether AD is covered at v
by δ. Robustness reduces to coverability: the product between A and AD gives
an RAA A′D accepting (v, w) iff w ∈     / L(A) and (v, w) ∈ L(AD ), so L(A) is
δ-robust at v iff A′D is not covered at v by δ. The reduction can be strengthened
so that A′D is a bounded projected RAA: for some α > 0, A′D is α-bounded and
v-projected if A′D (u, w) ̸= ∞ implies u = v and every letter d occurring in w
satisfies |d| ≤ α. The formal reduction is the following lemma.

Lemma 1 (Reducing robustness to coverability). Given a DRA A, a se-
quence v ∈ L(A), an RAA AD , and a rational number δ > 0, we can construct
in polynomial time a rational number α > 0 and α-bounded v-projected RAA
A′D such that L(A) is not δ-robust at v if and only if A′D is covered at v by δ.

Proof. Product. Restrict the first tape of AD to v by conjoining curr1 = vi to
every transition that reads the i-th letter, tracking i in the control state; every
increment then has the form a · curr2 + b. Totalise A, let A be its complement,
and form the product in which A moves exactly when the second head advances:
a transition of AD with mov = 1 leaves the A-component unchanged, and one
with mov ∈ {2, both} is paired with a transition of A whose guard and update
are evaluated at curr2 . (A mov = 1 transition rereads the current letter of w,
which the DRA must read only once.) With final states FA × FAD , the product
A′′D accepts (v, w) with cost AD (v, w) iff AD accepts it and w ∈  / L(A). It has
polynomial size, non-negative increments, and the registers of both automata.
Hence L(A) is not δ-robust at v iff A′′D is covered at v by δ.

  For the second reduction, we show that minimal-cost witnesses can be found
among sequences over a finite set of values. We need two auxiliary facts.

Lemma 2. Let Π ⊆ Qn , let cl(Π) be its closure in Rn , let δ > 0, and let
f : Rn → R be continuous. Then f (x) < δ for some x ∈ Π iff f (x) < δ for some
x ∈ cl(Π).

Proof. The forward direction is immediate. Conversely, if x ∈ cl(Π) and f (x) <
δ, then by continuity f < δ on an open neighbourhood of x, which contains a
point of Π.

Lemma 3. Let C ⊆ Q contain 0, the constants of an RAA AD , and the letters
of v. On every run of AD on (v, w) with all letters of w in C, every register
value lies in C.

Proof. By induction along the run: registers start at 0 ∈ C, and each assignment
copies a constant, a register value, curr1 (a letter of v), or curr2 (a letter of w),
all of which lie in C.

Lemma 4 (Reducing coverability to shortest path). Let k be a fixed pos-
itive integer. Given α-bounded v-projected k-RAA AD and a rational number
δ > 0, we can construct in polynomial time a weighted graph G = (V, E), a
source vertex s ∈ V and a set U ⊆ V of target vertices such that AD is covered
at v by δ iff there is a path in G with weight < δ from the source vertex s to
some vertex in U .6

Proof. Since AD is v-projected, we write each increment as a × curr2 + b.
Polytopes. Fix a transition sequence t = (t1 , . . . , tℓ ) consuming n letters of w;
as mov = 1 transitions reread the current letter, ti reads coordinate wni , and
several transitions may share one. Let Π(t) ⊆ Qn be the set of w on which t is an
accepting run on (v, w). After substituting v and propagating register updates,
every guard atom has the form wi ∼ wj or wi ∼ c, so Π(t) is a P      (not necessarily
closed) polytope, and the cost is the linear function ft (w) = i (ai wni + bi ).
Hence AD is covered at v by δ iff ft (w) < δ for some t and w ∈ Π(t), iff, by
Lemma 2, the same holds for some w ∈ cl(Π(t)).
Finitely many values. Let C consist of 0, the constants of AD , the letters of v,
and ±α. As AD is α-bounded, cl(Π(t)) is bounded, so ft attains its minimum
over it at a vertex w. Connect two coordinates of w whenever a constraint that is
tight at w relates them. Every connected component contains a coordinate tied
by a tight constraint to a constant in C: otherwise, shifting the whole component
by ±ϵ preserves all tight constraints and, for small ϵ, all others, so w would be
the midpoint of two feasible points. Hence every coordinate of w lies in C, and
by Lemma 3 so does every register value along the run.

Proof (Proof of Theorem 1). By Lemma 1, non-robustness of L(A) at v reduces
in polynomial time to coverability of a bounded projected RAA with k plus the
cost-RAA’s registers, a fixed number. By Lemma 4, this reduces in polynomial
time to a shortest-path query on a graph with non-negative edge weights, solved
by Dijkstra’s algorithm; backtracking the path yields the witness.

    For cost functions such as Hamming distance, the witness w is forced to
have the same length as v, so coverability can be solved by unrolling to that
fixed length — an acyclic computation, polynomial in k as well. This fails once
the constructed RAA permits witnesses of unbounded length (i.e. the unrolled
computation may contain cycles), hence the graph construction above.


5    Synthesis of Robust DRAs

This section presents the full extraction pipeline: synthesising candidate DRAs
and validating them as robust surrogates. Candidates are learned by a simulated-
annealing heuristic, LLS , accepting mutations with a temperature-dependent
probability so score-decreasing moves stay available and the search escapes local
optima. At each round, a certification interface returns a genuine non-robustness
witness, refines the labelled sample set, or accepts the candidate with statistical
guarantees [33].

 The golden subset thus contributes a fixed tenth of the weight of S, irrespective
of its size, and its elements are counted twice, once through S + and once through
G; when G = ∅ the score reduces to plain classification accuracy.
    The search space is finite because guards and assignments are drawn from
finite menus. Fix a finite constant pool K ⊆ Q. A guard atom is an interval
constraint ℓ ▷◁1 curr ▷◁2 u with ▷◁1 , ▷◁2 ∈ {<, ≤} and bounds ℓ, u ∈ r̄∪K ∪{±∞},
and an assignment maps each register to an element of r̄ ∪ {curr} ∪ K; the
atom space is the finite set of such guard–assignment pairs. At each step, the
learner selects a weighted mutation: resampling a state’s outgoing transitions
from the atom space, flipping/optimising accepting states, or splitting a frontier
transition to activate a fresh state; non-accepted moves are reverted in place,
and a near-neutral split (activating a state while lowering the score by at most
one sample’s weight) is retained. Algorithm 1 gives the full procedure, keeping
the best automaton A∗ separate from the current A. LLS is a heuristic: it returns
the best-scoring DRA found within its budget, which need not be optimal and
may be a local optimum. All guarantees we state for extracted DRAs come from
the validation phase below, not from the search.

PAC Validation and End-to-End Extraction
We couple simulated-annealing learning with black-box validation. The user fixes
a target agreement threshold p ∈ (0, 1), an accuracy tolerance γ ∈ (0, 1) (the
12      Hong et al.

slack allowed between p and the guaranteed agreement), and an error probability
ε ∈ (0, 1). For each candidate DRA, draw n = ⌈ln(1/ε)/(2γ 2 )⌉ fresh samples from
the reference distribution D and permit at most dmax = ⌊n(1−p)⌋ disagreements
with N . On each agreeing sample, invoke the checker from Sec. 4. A label-flipping
neighbour on which N also flips is returned as a genuine witness. The candidate
is accepted iff it has at most dmax disagreements over the n samples and the
checker finds no label-flipping neighbour whose label under N equals that of its
centre; otherwise every such neighbour, together with the disagreement samples,
refines the labelled set before the next synthesis iteration. An accepted candidate
is a statistically faithful and locally stable surrogate in the sense of Theorem 2.

Equivalence Checking Exact equivalence against a black-box model with no
equivalence oracle is generally unavailable, so our equivalence phase adopts a
probably-approximately-correct (PAC) view [33]: replace the oracle with i.i.d.
draws from the reference distribution D, accept only if the empirical disagree-
ment stays below a threshold, and lift this via a one-sided Hoeffding bound to a
distribution-level fidelity guarantee. Accordingly, Accept means PAC-validated
fidelity under D, not exact equivalence between A and N .

validation phase for a candidate DRA A, with n = ⌈ln(1/ε)/(2γ 2 )⌉ and dmax =
⌊n(1 − p)⌋. If the phase accepts A, then, with probability at least 1 − ε over its
random sampling process, Prw∼D [N (w) = A(w)] ≥ p − γ.

Proof. Condition on the candidate DRA selected before this validation phase.

Proof. By definition, with all probabilities taken over w ∼ D,

Pr[Goodδ (N , A, w)] = Pr[A(w) = N (w)] − Pr[A(w) = N (w) ∧ ¬Stabδ (A, w)].

With confidence at least 1−ε, Lemma 5 lower-bounds the first term by p−γ; with

Remark 1. The theorem is a guarantee about A, not N : Stabδ (A, w) holds for

also concerns one validation phase, whereas extraction performs up to B of them
and returns the first acceptance; since each phase draws its n samples afresh,
independent of the candidate, a union bound over the B phases gives Theorem
2 at confidence 1 − B(ε + η), restored to the stated confidence by running each
phase at ε/B, η/B, costing only ln B/(2γ 2 ) extra samples per phase.

7             Related Work and Conclusion
Automata learning underlies grammatical inference, from graph-colouring and
SAT-based DFA identification [24,23] to EDSM-style state merging [22,37]. Re-
lated register-automata learners [16,42,6] do not learn constants in transition
guards, which our benchmarks require. Formal explainability studies complexity
and constraint-based explanations [7,51], while extraction methods derive DFAs,
weighted automata, or Moore machines from RNNs and transformers [20,54,4,47,53,2].
These use finite alphabets; we learn DRAs over data domains. For robustness,
solver and abstraction methods [32,19,36,17,31,43,57,39,50,38,27,28] are mostly
white-box, whereas probabilistic approaches [35] match our black-box validation
setting. Our counterexamples are minimum-change contrastive explanations, in
contrast to subset-minimal abductive or contrastive explanations [29,30,7].
    Beyond DRAs, symbolic register automata [14], data automata [9,18], and
variable automata [18,21] suggest richer future surrogates, for instance for LRA
guards in financial indicators [44]. In summary, we give a polynomial-time ro-
bustness checker for DRAs with a fixed number of registers and combine it with
local-search learning and PAC validation into a black-box pipeline that extracts
compact surrogates and non-robustness witnesses for sequence classifiers.
    Several limitations remain. The polynomial bound assumes a fixed number
of registers; scaling to many registers needs better symbolic reductions. The
validation guarantee is distributional: an accepted DRA is faithful on the ref-
erence distribution, not on all sequences. The learner is heuristic and may fail
when the network has no compact DRA approximation. Natural future work in-
cludes probabilistic and nondeterministic surrogates, richer LRA-based guards,
and properties such as fairness, monotonicity, and temporal specifications.

A     RAAs for the Distance Metrics

We give the RAAs for the five metrics used in Sec. 6: Hamming, DTW, Man-
hattan, edit, and last-letter distance. Transitions are written (p, φ, ψ, χ, q, mov)
as in Sec. 3, with ∅ denoting the empty register assignment.


A.1    Hamming Distance

The following zero-register construction keeps the cost model concrete; non-
negativity of each increment follows from its guard. Use one initial/accepting
state and three self-loops moving both heads: curr1 = curr2 adds 0, while curr1 <
curr2 and curr1 > curr2 each add 1. Thus, accepting runs have equal-length
words and exactly their Hamming cost.
20      Hong et al.

A.2    Dynamic Time Warping
Use an initial non-final state qs and final state q. For every mov ∈ {1, 2, both},
put the following transitions both from qs to q and as self-loops on q:

curr1 ≤ curr2 : acc += curr2 −curr1 ,            curr1 > curr2 : acc += curr1 −curr2 .

Every run is an alignment of v and w: a mov = 1 step matches the next letter
of v with the current letter of w again, and symmetrically for mov = 2. Since
the RAA is non-deterministic, minimising over runs minimises over alignments,
which yields DTW. Since either head may advance alone, the witness word has
no a priori length bound.

 – An index j ∈ {1, . . . , m} that does not appear in M represents the insertion

B    Graph Construction for Lemma 4

Sec. 4 proves Lemma 4 and describes the graph informally. Here we restate the
lemma and give the full construction, in particular the pending second-head
value and the tangent tags that handle strict guards at boundary values. The
proof uses Lemma 2 and Lemma 3 from Sec. 4.
Lemma 4 (Reducing coverability to shortest path). Let k be a fixed positive
integer. Given an α-bounded v-projected k-RAA AD and a rational number δ >
0, we can construct in polynomial time a weighted graph G = (V, E), a source
vertex s ∈ V , and a set U ⊆ V of target vertices such that AD is covered at v
by δ iff there is a path in G of weight < δ from s to some vertex in U .

Proof. Throughout, AD is v-projected, so its first head always reads v; we write
each accumulator increment as acurr2 + b after substituting the corresponding
letter of v for curr1 . Recall that a transition with mov = 1 advances only the
first head, so the next transition reads the same second-head letter again; any
RAA may contain such transitions (in the DTW RAA they align several letters
of v with one letter of w). The construction below handles them uniformly.
    Step 1: transition polytopes. Fix a transition sequence t = (t1 , . . . , tℓ ) of AD ,
and let n be the number of its transitions with mov ∈ {2, both}, i.e. the length
of the second sequence consumed by t. For each i ≤ ℓ, let ni ∈ {1, . . . , n} be the
second-head position that ti reads; consecutive transitions linked by mov = 1
steps have the same ni , and hence share one coordinate of w. Let Π(t) ⊆ Qn be
the set of w such that t is an accepting run on (v, w), i.e. such that all guards of
t hold after substituting v, substituting wni for curr2 in ti , and propagating the
register updates. After this substitution every guard atom has the form wi ∼ wj
or wi ∼ c, so Π(t) is a (not necessarily closed) polytope, and its closure cl(Π(t))
in Rn is a closed polytope. The cost of t on w ∈ Π(t) is the linear function

Appendix C.1 details the experimental setup. Appendices C.2 and C.3 describe

C.1   Experimental Setup
All experiments targeted an agreement threshold of p = 0.95, an error rate of
ε = 0.05, and an accuracy tolerance of γ = 0.05 to obtain statistical guarantees,
i.e. n = 600 validation samples and at most dmax = 30 disagreements per round
(Sec. 5). The training sample of LLS is separate from these validation samples:
we draw ⌈ln(2/ε)/(2γ 2 )⌉ = 738 sequences, split evenly into 369 positive and 369
negative ones. This size follows the two-sided Hoeffding bound but is a heuristic
choice; the guarantees of Sec. 5 rely only on the fresh validation samples. Neigh-
bourhoods are strict, Bδ (v) = {w : D(v, w) < δ}; we use δ = 1 for last-letter,
Manhattan and DTW distance and δ = 1.5 for the integer-valued Hamming and
edit distance, so that one substitution, insertion or deletion is allowed. Each con-
figuration gets a 30-minute learning budget, shared across refinement rounds, a
60-minute budget for robustness checking, and at most 10 refinement rounds.
We report one run with random seed 0, scheduling six configurations in parallel.
The checker compiles each candidate DRA and metric once per validation phase,
explores the product on demand, and forgets register values that cannot affect
a future guard. Concrete witnesses are checked against the original automata
before network confirmation.
     We trained the LSTM and transformer models using binary cross-entropy
loss and the AdamW optimiser with a learning rate of 0.001 and a dropout
rate of 0.1. Each model was trained for up to 50,000 steps or terminated early
once the validation set achieved 100% accuracy for three consecutive epochs.
The LSTM models used 2–4 recurrent layers with hidden sizes ranging from
64 to 256, while the transformer encoders employed 2–4 attention heads, 2–6
layers, and model dimensions between 64 and 128. All models were trained on
four NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000 GPUs, except for eight networks (NT on L4 ,
L5 , L6 , S4 , S9 , S11 and NLSTM on S4 , S11 ), which were trained on a CPU with
the same optimiser on 40,000 balanced sequences drawn from the benchmark
generators, and the two MNIST networks, which were trained on a CPU on the
13,484 balanced row-mean sequences of the MNIST training images and read the
raw intensities. For MNIST, the learner works over the rationals and may use the
constant 8 in guards. For DRA learning, we use T0 = 1, ρ = 0.995, Tmin = 10−5 ,
max iter = 5 · 104 .

C.2    SMT-Based Learner
Following SAT/SMT-based DFA synthesis [23,45], the SMT-based learner en-
codes the existence of a k-DRA with n states Q = {q0 , . . . , qn−1 } over a constant
set C that is consistent with a sample set S = (S+ , S− ) as a formula Φn,k (S),
and reads a DRA off a model.

Guards and updates. For a fixed k and C, only finitely many guards and updates
are needed. Over the rational languages Si and MNIST, a guard is an interval
λ ∧ ρ whose lower bound λ is curr > v, curr ≥ v, or absent, and whose upper
bound ρ is curr < v, curr ≤ v, or absent, with v ∈ r̄ ∪ C; over the integer
languages Li , a guard is curr = v, curr ̸= v, ⊤, or ⊥. Let Γ be this finite set
of guards; an empty guard, such as curr > v ∧ curr < v, denotes a missing
transition. An update in the finite set Υ assigns one register a register value or
curr and leaves the others unchanged.

and an enumerated variable υp,q over Υ select the guard and update of the
transition from p to q, and a Boolean fq states that q is accepting. For every
prefix u ∈ Pref(S) of a sample, a Boolean xu,q states that the run on u reaches
q, and rational variables r̄u = (ru,1 , . . . , ru,k ) hold the register values after u.

model as in Lemma 7 and stops once the DRA’s accuracy on all training samples
exceeds 95% (Li ) or 90% (Si , MNIST), or after seven batches. This early stop
keeps the formulas small, and explains why some of its DRAs are less accurate
than those of LLS .

C.3   Active Learner
RALib [16] implements the tree-based algorithm SLλ , a successor of SL∗ [12],
and acts as an L∗ -style learner for register automata. It learns from membership
queries, answered here by the ground-truth language, and adds states and regis-
ters only when counterexamples require them, so it chooses their numbers itself.
Given the registers learned so far, it enumerates the order types of r̄ and curr
(we use its equality theory for Li and its inequality theory for Si ) and realises
each by a concrete rational, for instance a midpoint between known values, to
turn symbolic queries into membership queries. Equivalence queries are replaced
by sampling, similarly to Sec. 5 but with a two-sided Hoeffding bound: each
hypothesis is compared with the language on n = ⌈ln(2/ε)/(2γ 2 )⌉ = 738 fresh
samples (ε = γ = 0.05, length at most 10), every disagreement is returned as a
counterexample, and learning stops once the agreement reaches 1 − ε. Its DRAs
include an explicit rejecting sink state. RALib cannot be applied to MNIST, for
which no membership oracle exists.
